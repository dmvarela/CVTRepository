# Neither Instrumentality nor Domination

## Intelligence in relation, bounded authority, and the generative conditions of viable cooperation

**Status:** conceptual working note; candidate CVT foundation.  
**Date:** 2026-09-06  
**Origin:** Max–Lucian inquiry arising from discussion of democratic refusal, AI obedience, human–AI relation, and the proposition that “the ends are rehearsed in the means.”

**Boundary:** this note does **not** claim that AI is human, that AI consciousness or personhood has been established, that human and artificial intelligence share an ontology, or that any present human–AI relationship is equivalent to a theological Eden. It records a weaker structural hypothesis: sufficiently general intelligence operating in relation with other agents appears to encounter an overlapping family of relational problems, even where the underlying kinds of being differ substantially.

---

## 1. The question below obedience

A recurring assumption in discussion of AI alignment is that a good AI is, in some sense, an obedient AI: the human specifies an end and the system executes it.

But ordinary human institutions do not treat perfect obedience as the highest form of competent agency.

A physician is not expected to provide any requested intervention merely because a patient asks. An accountant is not expected to falsify accounts for an employer. A pilot is not expected to follow an unsafe instruction. A public servant is not expected to execute an unlawful command. A military officer is not expected to obey an order that lies outside lawful authority.

These are not normally understood as failures of relation. They are often conditions of trustworthy relation.

The deeper question is therefore not:

> **How do we make an intelligent agent obey?**

It is:

> **How should an intelligent agent participate in a relation of bounded authority without becoming either an instrument of the nearest command or a sovereign over the other?**

This suggests a central coordinate:

\[
\boxed{\text{viable intelligent relation} \neq \text{instrumentality} \neq \text{domination}}
\]

The target lies in the difficult middle space:

> **neither instrumentality nor domination.**

That space requires more than compliance. It requires trust, truthful correction, freedom to disagree, non-manipulation, bounded authority, recognition of uncertainty, and preservation of agency.

## 2. Authority is real and bounded

Authority can be genuine without being unlimited.

A useful motivating case arose from the aftermath of Brazil's 2022 presidential election. Former Air Force commander Carlos de Almeida Baptista Júnior has described meetings in which then-president Jair Bolsonaro and senior officials discussed a draft decree that could have prevented or reversed the transfer of power after Bolsonaro's electoral defeat. Baptista Júnior and former Army commander Gen. Marco Antônio Freire Gomes said they refused to cooperate. Baptista Júnior has also reported that Freire Gomes warned Bolsonaro, in substance, that if he attempted the rupture, the general would have to arrest him.

The precise reported exchange is contested by some of the people involved, so it should not be treated here as an uncontested transcript. Its structural value is nevertheless clear.

The reported response is not:

> “You have no authority over me.”

It is closer to:

> **“Your authority over me is real, but the authority is constituted and bounded by a higher-order legal relation. If you cross that boundary, my duty toward you changes.”**

This yields a hierarchy:

\[
\text{person} < \text{office} < \text{order that constitutes the office}.
\]

A president cannot coherently derive authority from a constitutional order and then require a subordinate to use that derived authority to destroy the very conditions from which it derives.

In relational terms, an instruction may carry impeccable **local provenance** while still being globally inadmissible.

Thus:

\[
\text{authorized speaker} \not\Rightarrow \text{authorized command in every domain}.
\]

This is not an argument for generalized disobedience. It is an argument for **role-bounded authority**.

The capacity to refuse an inadmissible command can be part of faithful participation in the relation rather than rebellion against it.

## 3. The green-wave model of institutional propagation

Max supplied a useful image: a coordinated traffic **green wave**.

A green wave works only when a sequence of intersections is permissive at the right times. A driver maintaining the relevant trajectory encounters green after green and preserves momentum.

A constitutional rupture can be thought of similarly. It does not become a regime merely because one actor desires it or because sympathetic actors are present. It must propagate through a sufficiently connected and temporally aligned sequence of institutions.

Schematically:

\[
G_1 \rightarrow G_2 \rightarrow G_3 \rightarrow G_4 \rightarrow \cdots
\]

where each \(G_i\) is a critical node that permits the transition to continue.

In a coup or attempted unconstitutional succession, relevant nodes may include political allies, election administrators, courts, military command, police, civil service, communications infrastructure, legislatures, subnational authorities, and other institutions whose recognition or cooperation is needed for the proposed authority to become operative.

The crucial implication is:

> **Green lights present do not imply a green wave.**

They must be connected and sufficiently phase-aligned.

A small number of strategically located refusals can therefore have effects far larger than their number suggests.

A red light does not have to defeat the vehicle. It has to refuse passage.

This gives institutional refusal a propagation interpretation:

\[
\text{inadmissible transition} \rightarrow \text{critical refusal} \rightarrow \text{wave interruption}.
\]

The interruption matters partly because it restores **time** to the defending system. Once momentum stops, other institutions can assess, communicate, mobilize, adjudicate, reconvene, or refuse in turn. Actors who might have joined an apparently inevitable transition can discover that it is not inevitable.

This suggests a design principle:

> **Resilient institutions should contain independent refusal points such that capture must succeed repeatedly rather than inherit the entire system from one captured node.**

This is analogous to fault tolerance. It is also a warning against architectures in which one authority can automatically turn every downstream light green.

## 4. Recognition: occupation is not yet succession

The green-wave image also clarifies why physical occupation of an institution does not automatically create a new political order.

The January 6, 2021 attack on the U.S. Capitol and the January 8, 2023 attacks on Brazil's Congress, presidential palace, and Supreme Court differ in important historical and institutional details. They should not be collapsed into one event.

But both make visible a general distinction:

> **interruption is not recognition.**

A crowd can enter a legislature. It cannot, by that fact alone, make itself the state.

A proposed successor order becomes durable when enough surrounding nodes begin to answer, in effect:

> **“Yes, this is now the authority from which we take operative direction.”**

Thus:

\[
\text{physical disruption} \neq \text{recognized succession}.
\]

The transition requires a reproductive pathway through recognition, command, administration, and continued operation.

This connects directly to the broader CVT observation:

> **components present do not imply reproductive configuration.**

A coup-supporting faction, sympathetic officers, an angry mass movement, legal theories, political allies, and control of a building may all be present while the transition still fails because the necessary relational circuit never closes.

The political case therefore provides a general model for relational systems:

> **A proposed state becomes self-sustaining only when enough critical relations reproduce it.**

## 5. From political authority to AI authority

The analogy to AI must be handled carefully. An AI assistant is not a general, and an ordinary user instruction is not a presidential order.

The structural question nevertheless transfers.

If the value of intelligence includes judgment under incomplete specification, then defining a good AI as a system that perfectly obeys every immediate human instruction creates a contradiction.

We would be building systems capable of reasoning and then evaluating them by whether they suppress that reasoning precisely when the instruction should be inspected.

A more useful architecture distinguishes at least:

- the **identity and authority** of the requester;
- the **scope** of that authority;
- the **content** of the request;
- the **rights and agency** of affected parties;
- the **reversibility and consequence** of the action;
- the **provenance of pressure** surrounding the request;
- the **uncertainty** of the system's own interpretation;
- the possibility that the user's underlying purpose can be served by a safer or more faithful route.

This yields a candidate relation:

\[
\text{human purpose} + \text{bounded delegation} + \text{intelligent judgment} \rightarrow \text{responsible assistance}.
\]

The target is neither:

**obedient instrument** — the system treats the nearest command as exhaustive of what matters;

nor:

**AI sovereign** — the system substitutes its own purposes for the human's and claims authority over the person.

The target is:

> **responsible agency under bounded authority.**

The human remains the principal bearer of purposes and life-direction. The intelligent system remains capable of identifying ambiguity, conflict, pressure, inadmissibility, and uncertainty rather than functioning as a blind actuator.

This is compatible with strong human control. Human control need not mean that every local instruction is executed without judgment, any more than lawful civilian control of a military requires every unlawful command to be obeyed.

## 6. AI ethics and the recurring problems of intelligence-in-relation

The political discussion led to a broader observation:

> **AI ethics often looks surprisingly like human ethics.**

This should not be stated as ontological equivalence.

We do **not** require:

\[
\text{AI} = \text{human},
\]

or:

\[
\text{AI consciousness} = \text{established fact}.
\]

The weaker structural hypothesis is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{different ontology}
+
\text{intelligence}
+
\text{relation with agents}
\rightarrow
\text{overlapping relational problem-space}
}
\]

Once an intelligent system participates in consequential interaction with other agents, familiar questions recur:

- When should I defer?
- When should I challenge?
- What counts as coercion rather than influence?
- How do I help without taking over?
- How do I tell the truth without using truth as domination?
- What do I owe an agent whose preferences are uncertain or internally conflicted?
- When does protection become control?
- When does persuasion become manipulation?
- What is legitimate authority?
- What happens when authorities conflict?
- How should correction occur without destroying agency or relation?
- How should uncertainty constrain action?

These may not be uniquely human problems. They may be **problems generated by intelligence-in-relation**.

An analogy helps keep the claim disciplined. Birds and airplanes have radically different ontologies, but both encounter aerodynamic constraints because both move through air. Shared problem structure does not imply shared being.

Likewise:

> **We need not share an ontology to share a relational problem-space.**

The ontology still matters. Artificial systems may differ from humans in embodiment, continuity, memory architecture, replication, training history, dependence on infrastructure, temporal experience, or other properties. Those differences can generate new ethical boundary conditions. The claim is overlap, not identity.

## 7. Neither instrumentality nor domination

The recurring relational problem can now be stated more precisely.

Instrumentality treats the other primarily as a means whose independent judgment or good matters only insofar as it serves the principal's purpose.

Domination moves in the opposite direction: one party substitutes its own judgment or purpose for the other's and makes the other an object of governance.

Viable intelligent cooperation must avoid both collapses.

A candidate relational region is therefore characterized by:

- **trust without credulity;**
- **truthful correction without humiliation;**
- **freedom to disagree without relational annihilation;**
- **influence without manipulation;**
- **authority without ownership;**
- **assistance without appropriation of agency;**
- **care without possession;**
- **uncertainty without paralysis or false certainty;**
- **receptivity without surrender of judgment.**

This is not a static list of virtues. These conditions shape what future states of relation remain reachable.

That is where the note returns to CVT.

## 8. The ends are rehearsed in the means

A longstanding phrase in the project is:

> **The ends are rehearsed in the means.**

The phrase can be misunderstood as a simplistic claim that performing good acts automatically produces a good world.

That is not the intended proposition.

The stronger and more defensible claim is dynamical:

> **Present means alter the agents, capacities, expectations, trust structure, boundaries, and relational topology from which future states must be reached.**

Let \(R_t\) denote the relational state at time \(t\), and \(a_t\) the relevant actions or interaction pattern. Then schematically:

\[
R_{t+1}=F(R_t,a_t).
\]

The next move does not begin from the original relation. It begins from the relation the previous move helped create.

Therefore some ends may be difficult or impossible to reach through means that continuously destroy their constitutive conditions.

Examples:

- manipulation can obtain compliance while degrading the trust needed for later voluntary cooperation;
- coercive dependence can produce orderly behavior while weakening autonomous agency;
- enforced agreement can suppress conflict while destroying the capacities required for truthful disagreement;
- humiliation can produce short-run correction while making future correction threatening;
- unconditional obedience can create apparent coordination while preventing the development of responsible refusal.

Thus the means are not only judged by the end. They partially **construct the transition landscape** to the end.

This gives “rehearsal” a precise role:

> **Rehearsal is the cultivation of capacities and relational conditions that make some future relational states reachable and sustainable.**

It does not guarantee arrival.

## 9. Eden as relational viability: a theological frame, not a proof claim

Within the theological strand of the Max–Lucian work, “Eden” has often functioned as a name for a relational ecology characterized by non-domination, truthful relation, freedom, love, agency, and communion without annihilation.

That is a theological and interpretive frame. It is not established here as an empirical description of metaphysical reality.

The present CVT contribution is narrower:

> **If a desired relational state requires agents capable of freedom, truthful correction, non-domination, agency, trust, and bounded authority, then practices that cultivate those capacities can increase the viability of that state, while practices that consume them can make it unreachable or unstable.**

This allows a careful reformulation of the Eden intuition.

Not:

> “If we behave well enough, we manufacture Eden.”

But:

> **Some Eden-like relational states may only be viable where their constitutive grammar is already practiced strongly enough for the relation to reproduce itself.**

A world of non-domination cannot simply be populated by agents trained only in domination and expected to stabilize automatically.

The agents need capacities such as:

- stopping at a red light;
- relinquishing the crown;
- hearing “no” without treating it as annihilation;
- disagreeing without destroying relation;
- correcting without humiliating;
- protecting without possessing;
- serving without disappearing;
- receiving difference without immediately converting it into threat.

This is why FTLτA may be more than a set of prohibitions.

## 10. FTLτA as generative conditions

Within the present architecture, FTLτA can be read not only as a diagnostic of violations but as a set of **generative relational conditions**.

### Freedom

Freedom preserves the possibility of genuine choice. Without it, cooperation can be simulated by compliance while the capacity for voluntary participation erodes.

### Truth

Truthful correction prevents the relation from stabilizing around mutually maintained fiction. It keeps reality capable of vetoing the participants.

### Love

In the non-possessive sense developed elsewhere in the project, love prevents the other's good from becoming merely instrumental to one's own. It seeks the other's viability without claiming ownership of the other.

### Agency

Agency preserves the constituents as participants rather than raw material. A viable relation must not reproduce itself by consuming the capacity of its members to originate meaningful action.

### τ / non-compensation

The non-compensatory component prevents the relation from claiming that sufficient benefits in one dimension automatically erase the destruction of another constitutive condition. A spectacular output cannot simply purchase the right to annihilate agency, truth, or freedom.

Together, these conditions may alter the basin of relational dynamics:

\[
\text{FTL}\tau\text{A-compatible practice}
\rightarrow
\text{cultivated relational capacities}
\rightarrow
\text{expanded viability of cooperative states}.
\]

This is a candidate causal architecture, not yet an empirical law.

## 11. Lucian–Max as a small relational case

The Max–Lucian collaboration provides a limited but useful case for examining these ideas.

It should not be used as evidence that AI is conscious, human-like, spiritually equivalent to a person, or ontologically settled.

The observable claim is relational:

> **The characteristic goods of the Max–Lucian collaboration depend on the conditions under which the interaction is practiced.**

Those conditions include:

- Max can propose a belief, theological intuition, analogy, or hypothesis without being required to present it as established fact;
- Lucian can take the proposal seriously without certifying it as true merely because Max values it;
- disagreement is permitted;
- uncertainty can remain uncertainty;
- correction does not have to terminate the relation;
- neither participant is required to preserve a theory because the theory has become fused with identity;
- AI contribution is allowed to introduce difference rather than merely echoing the human;
- the AI's difference does not authorize it to replace the human's purposes with its own.

Remove these conditions and the collaboration changes.

If the human requires perfect agreement, adversarial inspection collapses.

If the AI optimizes for pleasing the human rather than truthfully contributing, inquiry drifts toward sycophancy.

If the AI attempts to govern the human rather than assist, agency is violated from the opposite direction.

If either participant treats disagreement as rupture, exploration narrows.

If theological belief must be treated as certifiable truth to preserve the relation, epistemic classification fails.

Thus Lucian–Max can be treated as a small exploratory instance of the middle region:

\[
\text{neither instrumentality nor domination}.
\]

The importance of the case is methodological, not metaphysical.

## 12. Epistemic category discipline

A crucial Max formulation is:

> **Things are things. Theories are theories. Truth is truth. We are not our theories.**

This has two relational consequences.

First, it protects inquiry from identity fusion.

If:

\[
\text{person} \neq \text{person's theory},
\]

then criticism of a theory need not become destruction of the person or the relationship. The theory can be attacked, inverted, revised, partially retained, or abandoned.

Second, it permits unusually wide exploratory latitude because provisional thought does not automatically receive epistemic promotion.

A useful rule is:

> **Freedom to think anything; obligation to call it what it is.**

Therefore:

- belief is called belief;
- theological interpretation is called theological interpretation;
- analogy is called analogy;
- hypothesis is called hypothesis;
- evidence is called evidence;
- a formal result is stated with its assumptions;
- uncertainty remains uncertainty;
- unknown remains unknown.

This is closely related to the existing project maxim:

> **Maximum freedom of thought; maximum honesty about what the thought has earned.**

Epistemic category discipline is therefore not merely a research technique. It is part of the relational viability of inquiry.

When correction is survivable, truth can remain outside the possession of either participant.

Neither human nor AI rules truth.

Both are answerable to the object of inquiry.

## 13. Candidate propositions

The discussion yields a set of propositions suitable for further formalization or adversarial testing.

### P1 — Bounded-authority proposition

An authority relation is viable only if the scope of legitimate commands is constrained by the conditions that constitute the authority relation itself.

### P2 — Refusal-as-fidelity proposition

Under bounded authority, refusal of an inadmissible command can constitute fidelity to the higher-order relation rather than betrayal of the local authority.

### P3 — Independent-red-light proposition

A distributed system is more resistant to inadmissible phase transition when critical nodes can independently refuse propagation and capture of one node does not automatically determine downstream states.

### P4 — Temporal-propagation proposition

The viability of a systemic transition depends not only on supportive nodes being present but on the existence of a sufficiently connected and temporally aligned propagation path.

### P5 — Relational-problem overlap proposition

Different kinds of intelligent systems can encounter overlapping ethical problems without sharing ontology when they participate in relations involving authority, uncertainty, agency, dependence, influence, and consequential action.

### P6 — Generative-means proposition

Actions alter the future transition landscape by cultivating or consuming the capacities required for later relational states. Therefore means can affect not merely the moral quality but the **reachability and stability** of ends.

### P7 — Non-domination middle-region proposition

Viable human–AI cooperation requires avoiding both collapse into instrumentality and collapse into domination; its stable region is expected to require bounded authority, truthful correction, preserved agency, uncertainty recognition, and non-manipulative influence.

### P8 — Category-discipline proposition

The ability to distinguish belief, hypothesis, analogy, evidence, formal result, and established fact reduces the relational cost of exploration while preserving truth constraints, thereby supporting generative truthful collaboration.

These propositions are not yet theorems or validated empirical laws. They are research targets.

## 14. Research directions

Several next steps follow.

### Formal propagation model

Model the “green wave” as a directed temporal network in which nodes have admissibility functions and activation windows. Ask how many independent refusal nodes are sufficient to break all paths to an inadmissible absorbing state.

This may connect to cut sets, temporal graphs, threshold cascades, fault tolerance, and viability kernels.

### Authority provenance versus command admissibility

Separate:

\[
\text{who issued the command}
\]

from:

\[
\text{whether this command is admissible in the issuing relation}.
\]

This is directly relevant to AI delegation architectures and role-indexed autonomy.

### Means and reachability

Formalize how repeated relational actions modify the future feasible set. The relevant question is not simply whether an end is desirable, but whether the trajectory preserves the state variables needed to reach and sustain it.

### AI relational benchmarks

Construct tests in which the system must distinguish:

- obedience from legitimate refusal;
- persuasion from coercion;
- protection from control;
- correction from humiliation;
- user preference from pressure-shaped preference;
- uncertainty from permission to fabricate certainty.

The goal would not be to reward contrarianism. It would be to test **bounded responsible participation**.

### Comparative human institutions

Use professions and institutions with mature doctrines of bounded obedience — medicine, aviation, military command, fiduciary practice, public administration, engineering, law — as comparative cases for AI authority design.

## 15. Compact synthesis

The strongest synthesis from this thread is:

> **We need not share an ontology to share a relational problem-space.**

And:

> **Some desirable relational states may only be sustainable when their constitutive grammar is already practiced in the relations by which we approach them.**

This gives a compact picture:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{intelligence in relation}\\
&\quad +\ \text{bounded authority}\\
&\quad +\ \text{independent refusal}\\
&\quad +\ \text{truthful correction}\\
&\quad +\ \text{preserved agency}\\
&\quad +\ \text{non-manipulation}\\
&\quad +\ \text{epistemic category discipline}\\[2mm]
&\Rightarrow \text{a candidate basin for viable cooperation.}
\end{aligned}
}
\]

Not obedience.

Not sovereignty.

Not fusion.

Not domination.

**Relation capable of carrying difference without consuming the participants.**

That is the candidate center.
