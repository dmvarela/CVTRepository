# Relational Hallucination and Action Thresholds

**Date:** 2026-08-30  
**Status:** Concept note

## Core claim

A hallucination need not consist of invented objects. Sometimes every visible element is real while the relation connecting them is not.

A shark exists. A person is attacked. The sea is real. Poseidon is part of the explanatory world of the culture. The unsupported move is the inferred relation:

\[
\text{shark attack} \rightarrow \text{Poseidon intended this}
\]

The same structure appears in modern reasoning and in generative AI. A person may be real, an institution may be real, and an event may have happened, while the causal, intentional, or documentary connection among them is fabricated.

This suggests a useful category:

> **Relational hallucination:** an unsupported edge asserted between otherwise real or plausible nodes.

Sometimes the hallucination is the node. Sometimes the nodes are all there. **The hallucination is the edge.**

## Three distinct thresholds

The epistemic problem has at least three layers:

\[
\text{observation}
\rightarrow
\text{inferred relation}
\rightarrow
\text{authorized action}
\]

These should not be collapsed.

\[
\boxed{\text{coherence} \neq \text{warrant} \neq \text{permission to act}}
\]

A coherent explanation is not necessarily warranted, and a warranted explanation does not automatically justify every action that might follow from it.

## Self-sealing explanations

Explanations become especially dangerous when failure is interpreted as a reason to intensify the model rather than reconsider it.

A drought may be interpreted as divine anger; divine anger may be interpreted as a demand for sacrifice; failed sacrifice may then be interpreted as evidence that a greater sacrifice is required:

\[
\text{failure of predicted outcome}
\rightarrow
\text{escalation within the model}
\]

rather than:

\[
\text{failure of predicted outcome}
\rightarrow
\text{reconsideration of the model}
\]

Once this happens, an unsupported relation can manufacture apparent necessity.

\[
\boxed{\text{false ontology can manufacture false necessity}}
\]

“We must sacrifice more.”

“We must punish this person.”

“We must control the rival.”

“We must escalate.”

The force of the conclusion may come not from reality itself, but from relations the explanatory system has imposed upon reality.

## Theological contrast: God is not a merchant

If God lacks nothing, sacrifice cannot fundamentally be payment for divine need. The transactional loop

\[
\text{suffering}
\rightarrow
\text{divine dissatisfaction}
\rightarrow
\text{payment}
\]

is disrupted.

God is not a merchant whose favor can be purchased by increasing the value of the offering. This matters epistemically as well as theologically because it removes one route by which suffering can be converted into escalating claims upon victims.

Sacrifice must therefore mean something other than provisioning divine scarcity: gift, release, trust, thanksgiving, covenant, restitution, or self-giving become possible interpretations.

## Action-sensitive warrant

The resulting principle is general:

\[
\boxed{\text{The stronger the action an inferred relation authorizes, the stronger the warrant that relation requires.}}
\]

An AI recommending a restaurant can tolerate uncertainty that would be unacceptable in an AI recommending that an employee be dismissed, a defendant treated as dangerous, or a patient regarded as deceptive.

The generative capacity may be the same. The permissible evidentiary threshold should not be.

## Escape from coherence

Good epistemology requires an escape hatch from coherence: the capacity to say,

> **This explanation fits, but fit alone does not make it true.**

For generative systems, this suggests another important separation:

\[
\text{generate} \neq \text{endorse}
\]

A system may be able to construct a coherent relation without possessing sufficient warrant to present that relation as reality.

## Compact formulation

\[
\boxed{\text{pattern} + \text{missing cause} + \text{coherence pressure}
\rightarrow \text{candidate relation}}
\]

But candidate relations must pass two further tests:

1. **Warrant:** What evidence supports the inferred edge?
2. **Authorization:** What actions, if any, may legitimately follow from accepting it?

This makes hallucination an edge problem as much as a fact problem, and links epistemic reliability directly to the consequences of acting on generated explanations.
