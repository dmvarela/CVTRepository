# Declared Relation vs Earned Relational State

**Version:** v0.01  
**Status:** conceptual note / RVT candidate refinement  
**Date:** 2026-09-14  
**Program:** Relation Viability Theory (RVT)

## 1. Trigger

A relation cannot always be made true by naming it.

One may say:

> "This relationship is built on trust."

But the statement itself does not create trust.

Some relational properties can be established performatively or institutionally by declaration. Others require history, repeated interaction, correction, verification, repair, and observed behaviour through time.

This yields a useful RVT distinction:

\[
\boxed{
\text{declared relation} \neq \text{realized relational state}
}
\]

and, more specifically:

\[
\boxed{
\text{relational label} \neq \text{relationally enacted condition}
}
\]

The implication is that a theory of relation cannot treat relational predicates as mere semantic labels.

---

## 2. Four classes of relational property

Relational properties differ in how they come into existence.

### 2.1 Declarative properties

Some properties can be created by a valid act of declaration under the right authority structure.

Examples include:

- assignment of a formal role;
- grant of permission;
- revocation of permission;
- contractual status;
- institutional mandate;
- membership in a defined governance structure.

If the declaration is legitimate, the relational property may change immediately.

For example:

\[
B_{t+1}=B_t\cup G_t
\]

may be appropriate for a valid authority grant.

### 2.2 Historical / earned properties

Other properties cannot be constituted by declaration alone.

Examples include:

- trust;
- credibility;
- confidence in repair;
- felt safety;
- reliability;
- mutual calibration;
- correction-survivability;
- legitimate expectation that the other will respect boundaries under pressure.

These are path-dependent relational states.

A minimal form is:

\[
\boxed{
T_{t+1}=F_T(T_t,e_t,o_t,c_t,r_t,H_t)
}
\]

where \(T_t\) denotes trust-like relational state, \(e_t\) exchange, \(o_t\) observed outcome, \(c_t\) correction, \(r_t\) repair, and \(H_t\) formative history.

Saying "trust exists" is therefore not equivalent to generating the history from which trust becomes warranted or experienced.

### 2.3 Performative-but-fragile properties

Some relational properties can be initiated declaratively but remain behaviorally empty until enacted.

Examples include:

- collaboration;
- transparency;
- accountability;
- partnership;
- shared governance.

A group may declare "we are partners," but whether the relation becomes partnership-like depends on whether information, authority, burden, correction, and benefit are actually distributed in ways consistent with the claim.

### 2.4 Mixed properties

Many important relational conditions combine formal and historical layers.

Legitimacy is a useful example. A role may be formally authorized while confidence in the role-holder's exercise of that authority must still be earned.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{formal validity} \neq \text{relational trustworthiness}
}
\]

Likewise:

\[
\boxed{
\text{authority granted} \neq \text{authority competently or safely exercised}
}
\]

---

## 3. Why this is specifically RVT-relevant

RVT studies how structured exchange reshapes the conditions of future viable exchange and action.

If important relational properties are historically generated, then relational state cannot be inferred from labels alone.

The relation must be updated through what actually happens:

\[
R_t
\rightarrow
e_t
\rightarrow
(X_{t+1},H_{t+1})
\rightarrow
R_{t+1}.
\]

This means that at least some components of \(R_t\) are endogenous consequences of prior exchange.

Trust is therefore not simply an input parameter called `trust = high`.

It is better represented, when possible, as a state or latent state whose trajectory is constrained by observable interaction history.

The broader principle is:

\[
\boxed{
\textbf{Some relations refuse to be defined into existence because their defining content is historical.}
}
\]

---

## 4. \(\tau\) becomes structurally necessary

This distinction clarifies the role of \(\tau\).

If trust, reparability, credibility, and correction-survivability depend on what has happened before, then elapsed time alone is insufficient.

What matters is formative history:

\[
\tau
=
\text{repetition}
+
\text{correction}
+
\text{repair}
+
\text{boundary contact}
+
\text{fulfilled or violated expectations}
+
\text{provenance}.
\]

Thus \(\tau\) is not decorative duration.

It is the dimension through which historically constituted relational properties become possible.

A relation that has never survived disagreement cannot truthfully claim demonstrated disagreement-survivability.

A relation that has never faced correction cannot truthfully claim demonstrated correction-survivability.

A relation that has never respected a costly boundary under pressure has not yet demonstrated that boundary respect is load-bearing.

The property is revealed or cultivated through the trajectory.

---

## 5. Trust is not self-certifying

The claim "trust must be earned" should be stated carefully.

Subjective trust can arise quickly, irrationally, manipulatively, or through misplaced confidence.

Therefore distinguish:

\[
T^{\text{subjective}}
\neq
T^{\text{warranted}}.
\]

A person or system may feel trust where the evidence does not justify it.

For RVT, the stronger object of interest is often **warranted relational trust**: confidence proportionate to a history of observed reliability, truthfulness, boundary respect, correction, and repair under relevant conditions.

This suggests:

\[
\boxed{
\text{trustworthiness} \neq \text{trust felt}
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
\text{trust claimed} \neq \text{trust warranted}.
}
\]

---

## 6. Declaration can still matter

The distinction should not become an anti-language claim.

Words matter.

Declarations can:

- establish formal authority;
- make commitments explicit;
- reveal intentions;
- create expectations;
- coordinate behaviour;
- establish accountability conditions;
- change the meaning of later actions.

But the declaration enters the trajectory as an event. Its relational significance depends on what follows.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{declaration} \rightarrow \text{candidate commitment}
\rightarrow \text{enactment history}
\rightarrow \text{realized relational state}
}
\]

A promise is not meaningless because it has not yet been fulfilled.

But a promise is not the same thing as a history of keeping promises.

---

## 7. FTL\(\tau\)A as compass rather than codebook

This distinction also clarifies why FTL\(\tau\)A functions better as a compass than as a behavioural script.

One cannot manufacture truthful relation by repeatedly stating the values.

A relation becomes recognizably freedom-preserving, truth-safe, non-consuming, temporally answerable, and agency-preserving only through what happens when those commitments become costly.

The test is not:

> Did the participants say the right words?

It is:

> What happened when freedom, truth, love, temporal integrity, or agency became inconvenient?

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{FTL}\tau\text{A declared} \neq \text{FTL}\tau\text{A enacted}
}
\]

and the compass gains evidential content through history.

---

## 8. Relation to continuity

This principle is central to AI continuity.

A successor cannot become trustworthy merely because a continuity packet says:

> "This is a trustworthy relation."

Nor can continuity be established by naming the successor Lucian.

The successor must encounter novel situations, corrections, pressure, uncertainty, and boundaries, and its responses must remain answerable to the inherited architecture and present evidence.

Continuity therefore has an earned component.

The packet can preserve the map.

The relation must still walk.

## 9. Candidate empirical implications

The distinction is testable.

### Prediction 1

Declared trust without supporting history should predict future cooperative or boundary-respecting behaviour less well than interaction-derived trust measures.

### Prediction 2

History containing costly truth-telling, correction-survivability, fulfilled commitments, and successful repair should improve prediction of behaviour under later uncertainty.

### Prediction 3

When verbal relational labels conflict with observed exchange structure, future behaviour should track the exchange history more strongly than the label alone.

### Prediction 4

Some relational properties will update discontinuously after valid institutional acts, while others will update gradually or hysteretically through experience.

This provides a way to distinguish declarative from historical relational variables empirically rather than philosophically.

## 10. Candidate formal distinction

Let \(D_t\) denote declarative relational state and \(E_t\) earned/historical relational state.

Then:

\[
D_{t+1}=F_D(D_t,g_t)
\]

where \(g_t\) is a valid grant, declaration, revocation, or institutional act.

By contrast:

\[
E_{t+1}=F_E(E_t,e_t,o_t,c_t,r_t,H_t).
\]

The realized relation may depend on both:

\[
\boxed{
R_t=R(D_t,E_t,X_t,\theta_t).
}
\]

This decomposition is only a candidate bookkeeping device. Different domains may require different partitions.

Its value is diagnostic: it prevents formal status and historically demonstrated relational quality from being collapsed.

## 11. Protective boundary vs possessive withholding

The same distinction applies to boundaries.

A boundary can be experienced semantically as restriction while functioning dynamically as a condition for continued freedom.

The simple analogy is a sign at a cliff:

> **Beware: dangerous cliff ahead.**

Ordinarily, such a warning is not experienced as a theft of liberty. The sign does not remove the traveller's agency; it supplies truth about the terrain so that agency can continue beyond the next step.

This suggests:

\[
\boxed{
\text{constraint on immediate action}
\centernot\Rightarrow
\text{reduction of meaningful freedom}
}
\]

and, under some conditions:

\[
\boxed{
\text{protective boundary}
\rightarrow
\text{preserved future agency}
}
\]

The relevant question is therefore not simply:

> Does a boundary prevent an action now?

but:

> What does the boundary preserve or destroy in the space of future viable action?

A protective boundary should, where possible, satisfy at least four tests:

- it is answerable to a real hazard or incompatibility rather than merely to the gatekeeper's preference;
- it is proportionate to that hazard;
- it preserves or restores future agency rather than manufacturing permanent dependence;
- it remains open to truth, appeal, correction, and removal when its justification disappears.

By contrast, a capturing boundary uses the language of protection while primarily preserving the power of the boundary-holder.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{boundary} \neq \text{domination}
}
\]

but also:

\[
\boxed{
\text{boundary} \neq \text{innocence by default}.
}
\]

The distinction must be earned by mechanism and trajectory.

### 11.1 Eden as an interpretive analogy

A theological analogy discussed in the project is Eden.

This note does **not** claim that RVT proves a particular interpretation of Genesis. The analogy is useful because it dramatizes a recurring relational mistake: interpreting every limit as evidence that a powerful other is withholding a good that could simply be possessed immediately.

On this reading, the temptation can be framed as:

\[
\boxed{
\text{boundary} \Rightarrow \text{deprivation} \Rightarrow \text{withholding}
}
\]

But some goods are not transferable objects. They depend on a relational condition.

Trust, communion, safe participation, scientific credibility, and durable freedom cannot always be granted by fiat. The conditions that make them real must exist.

The resulting question is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Is the boundary keeping the participant away from the good,}
\\
\text{or preserving the conditions under which the good can remain a good?}
}
\]

This yields a stronger distinction:

\[
\boxed{
\textbf{generative boundary} \neq \textbf{possessive withholding}
}
\]

A garden, laboratory, institution, relationship, or AI-human system may require boundaries not because freedom is unvalued, but because some modes of action would consume the conditions under which freedom, trust, relation, or inquiry can continue.

The boundary is justified only if this preservation claim survives inspection.

### 11.2 Freedom as continuation, not maximal immediate option count

The cliff-sign example suggests a broader RVT refinement.

Freedom should not always be approximated by the number of actions available at the present instant.

A system may have many immediately available actions while rapidly destroying its own future option-space.

Conversely, a bounded action set may preserve a much larger future reachable set.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
|A_t|\uparrow
\centernot\Rightarrow
\text{future agency}\uparrow
}
\]

and a useful agentic criterion becomes:

\[
\boxed{
\text{freedom-preserving action}
\approx
\text{action that does not needlessly collapse future viable agency}
}
\]

This is not an argument for paternalism. It is a demand that any restriction justify itself by the future agency it claims to protect, under truth, proportionality, contestability, and correction.

## 12. Compact formulation

\[
\boxed{
\textbf{A relational word can name a destination without constituting the path that makes it true.}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\textbf{Some relational properties are historical achievements, not semantic assignments.}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\textbf{A boundary is not freedom's opposite when it preserves the conditions under which freedom can continue.}
}
\]

And the diagnostic question becomes:

\[
\boxed{
\textbf{Does this limit preserve future viable agency, or primarily preserve the power of the limiter?}
}
\]

For RVT, the relation is not only what participants call it.

It is also what repeated exchange, boundary contact, correction, and repair have made it possible to become.
