\begin{abstract}
Industrial policy is often evaluated by whether a supported industry survives after protection is withdrawn. This paper argues that survival is an intermediate criterion rather than the terminal one. We model productive development as a staged process of activation, capability accumulation, graduation, generativity, and structural replacement. An initially nonviable productive relation can operate under temporary support while accumulating durable capability. In the baseline law $q_{t+1}=(1-\delta)q_t+\lambda$, unsupported viability requires $q_t\ge q^*$; for $0<\delta<1$, finite graduation is possible if and only if $\lambda/\delta>q^*$, yielding a closed-form minimum support duration. We then allow capability conversion and unsupported thresholds to depend on surrounding productive structure, distinguish durable capability from contemporaneous dependence and from the causal provenance of learning, and analyze a positive coupled capability system $q_{t+1}=Mq_t+b$. An unsupported dependency closure prevents false graduation through currently support-dependent neighbors. Because instantaneous withdrawal survival need not imply durability, we add a dynamic test: for a fixed support-free configuration, $M_0\vartheta+b_0\ge\vartheta$ is sufficient for the capability-viability orthant to be forward invariant. A political-economy extension distinguishes credibility that the announced support window will be honored from credibility that its terminal condition cannot be privately renegotiated. Under monotone reduced-form route values $G(\sigma)$ and $D(\chi)$, the region in which graduation is privately preferred to political dependence is upward closed in both credibility dimensions. Finally, we distinguish configuration-specific from recombinable capability and separate failed graduation, destructive de-accretion, competitive displacement, and successful supersession. The contribution is deliberately bounded: to connect temporary support, credible graduation, network-conditioned capability, dynamic unsupported viability, and post-exit capability retention in one diagnostic framework.
\end{abstract}
