# Paper F Design Note — The Playing Field, Discipline, and Development

## Why this note exists

A central mechanism emerged from comparing South Korea, Chile, Argentina, the decline of Korean footwear, and the 2026 FATE/Pampa Energía episode.

The core insight is that development does not require policy constancy. It requires a sufficiently credible **rule governing change**.

> **The developmental state should stabilize the rules of change, not stabilize the existing composition of production.**

This note is a design input for Paper F, not yet a finished theorem or literature claim.

---

## 1. The playing field

A stable playing field is not one in which tariffs, firms, industries, or policies never change. It is one in which agents can understand the rule by which they will change.

A tariff path such as

`30% -> 20% -> 10% -> 0%`

may be more institutionally stable than a tariff held at 30% for years but subject to sudden discretionary reversal.

So:

> **policy stability != policy constancy**

A developmental playing field provides:

- known performance criteria;
- credible consequences for failure;
- credible support while conditions are met;
- a legible path to withdrawal or graduation;
- enough durability for long-horizon investment;
- enough adaptability for firms, sectors, and workers to move.

The field remains; the players may change.

---

## 2. Discipline as a developmental mechanism

The South Korean case suggests that temporary support works differently when firms believe that protection, credit privileges, or other support can actually be withdrawn.

The crucial object is therefore not only support today, but the expected rule for support tomorrow.

A stylized capability law remains:

`q_(t+1) = (1-delta) q_t + lambda_t`

but the firm's behavior during the supported period depends on whether it expects graduation to be enforced.

The discipline condition can be expressed informally as:

> **The credible removal of protection is part of the protection policy itself.**

If firms believe that poor performance will automatically produce renewed protection, then the protected period need not generate urgency to learn, invest, export, or upgrade.

If withdrawal is credible, the entire optimization problem changes before the deadline arrives.

---

## 3. Rents can finance capability or capture

Protection creates rents. Those rents can be used in at least two broad ways:

`R_t = k_t + l_t`

where:

- `k_t` = productive investment: technology, learning, scale, suppliers, quality, export capability;
- `l_t` = political investment: lobbying, influence, campaign support, regulatory capture, efforts to preserve protection.

This creates a political-economy trap:

`protection -> rents -> political power -> more protection`

A developmental regime must make productive performance a more reliable route to continued support than political influence.

A useful stylized distinction is:

**Developmental regime**

`d s_(t+1) / d q_t  >>  d s_(t+1) / d l_t`

**Captured regime**

`d s_(t+1) / d l_t  >>  d s_(t+1) / d q_t`

The second regime encourages firms to optimize for political survival rather than graduation.

This suggests a candidate Paper F proposition:

> **A graduation regime is credible only if firms cannot convert the rents created by temporary protection into enough political influence to prevent graduation.**

This should be treated as a hypothesis/design target until formally modeled and situated in the literature.

---

## 4. Embedded autonomy, not state-business separation

The Korean lesson is not that the state was detached from firms. A developmental state must know what firms need, where bottlenecks lie, what foreign buyers demand, and which capabilities are missing.

The desired condition is closer to **embedded autonomy**:

> connected enough to industry to learn, autonomous enough that individual incumbents cannot purchase exemption from the performance rule.

This distinction matters because total bureaucratic insulation creates ignorance, while capture converts developmental policy into incumbent protection.

Relevant literature to audit carefully: Alice Amsden on reciprocity/performance discipline; Peter Evans on embedded autonomy; work on Korean directed credit, export discipline, chaebol-state relations, and later capture.

---

## 5. Exit is not one thing

Paper F should preserve the distinction among at least four outcomes:

1. **Failed graduation** — support ends and the capability cannot survive unsupported.
2. **Graduation and persistence** — capability survives exposure and continues.
3. **Successful supersession** — the economy outgrows the activity, while useful capabilities transfer or recombine elsewhere.
4. **Destructive de-accretion** — loss of one productive relation reduces the viability of connected relations and thins the basin.

South Korean footwear may illustrate supersession/structural transformation rather than simple developmental failure.

The FATE/Pampa Energía episode is useful as a candidate de-accretion edge, but must not be overclaimed: the case requires careful causal documentation and a full welfare analysis before normative conclusions.

---

## 6. Stable rules, changing production

The Chile-Argentina contrast helps define the playing field.

The relevant distinction is not "one country changes policy and the other does not." Both change policy.

The deeper question is whether firms and households can form long-horizon expectations about:

- monetary and exchange-rate rules;
- access to inputs and foreign currency;
- tariff and tax trajectories;
- contract enforcement;
- conditions for support and withdrawal;
- what can and cannot be renegotiated politically.

A country may therefore have a stable playing field while policies change substantially, if the **rule of adaptation** is credible.

This suggests:

> **Development requires disciplined change inside a credible institutional frame.**

Too much rigidity produces stagnation.

Too much discretion produces capture and short horizons.

Too little support can prevent capability formation.

Too much support can prevent graduation.

A useful verbal summary is:

> **support enough to learn, pressure enough to adapt, stability enough to plan.**

## 7. Human transition: discipline firms, do not abandon people

The worker side should probably remain a policy/welfare extension rather than enter the core theorem, but it is conceptually important.

The state need not preserve every firm or job in order to avoid abandoning people affected by structural change.

The clean distinction is:

> **worker protection != job protection**

A developmental transition regime can combine:

- hard budget/exit discipline for firms;
- real, portable transition opportunities for people;
- freedom to choose among paths rather than assignment to a state-selected destination.

The relevant object is not the nominal opportunity set but the **feasible opportunity set**.

Let `A` be all formally available options, and let `R(a_i)` be the money, time, credentials, geography, childcare, information, and risk required to execute option `a_i`. Given a person's resources `r`, define:

`A^F(r) = {a_i in A : R(a_i) <= r}`

A government can advertise many programs while leaving `|A^F| = 0` for the displaced person.

This suggests a second institutional principle:

> **The state owes pathways, not destinations.**

Potential instruments include portable retraining grants, income support while studying, recognition of prior learning, employer-linked transition programs, relocation assistance, and credible routes into expanding sectors. These should expand feasible agency without forcing a particular occupation or geography.

This is the human counterpart to firm discipline:

> **credible exit for firms + credible alternatives for people**

## 8. CVT / FTLtauA connection

The development problem has a continuity structure:

> How can a productive system change without consuming the relations and people that make continued transformation possible?

This is why development has a CVT-like form: stable relation/rule structure with changing constituents.

The FTLtauA interpretation should remain analytically disciplined and probably appear as a normative extension rather than as an empirical premise:

- **Truth:** do not pretend permanently nonviable firms are viable.
- **Freedom:** do not freeze people into an obsolete productive map.
- **Love / non-abandonment:** aggregate gains do not make concentrated human losses irrelevant.
- **Agency:** people choose among genuinely executable transition paths.
- **tau / non-compensation boundary:** aggregate welfare gains alone do not justify treating affected people as disposable residuals.

A compact formulation:

> **Do not freeze the productive map to protect people. Build bridges so people can cross when the map changes.**

## 9. Candidate formal extensions for Paper F

Do not add all of these automatically. Stress-test first.

1. **Anticipated withdrawal:** make current capability investment depend on the expected future support rule.
2. **Rent allocation:** allow protection rents to be divided between capability investment and political capture.
3. **Capture threshold:** characterize when political influence makes announced graduation non-credible.
4. **Rule credibility:** distinguish policy level from the meta-rule governing policy change.
5. **Negative linkage dynamics:** allow de-accretion, crowding out, and competitive displacement rather than imposing universal complementarity.
6. **Exit classification:** distinguish failed graduation, persistence, supersession, and destructive de-accretion.
7. **Human transition extension:** define feasible agency separately from firm/sector viability.

## Strong working claims to preserve

> **Time under protection is not learning.**

> **The credible removal of protection is part of the protection policy itself.**

> **Development requires the ability to disappoint incumbents.**

> **Policy stability is not policy constancy.**

> **The developmental state should stabilize the rules of change, not stabilize the existing composition of production.**

> **Protect valuable capabilities from needless destruction; do not protect firms from failure.**

> **The field remains. The players change.**

> **Real transition policy expands the feasible opportunity set; it does not merely advertise nominal options.**

- Audit the Korean literature for the exact institutional mechanisms that made withdrawal and performance discipline credible, and for counterexamples where political influence overrode performance.
- Compare Korea with Latin American protection regimes where rents became politically self-preserving.
- Separate empirically the Chilean "credible field" claim from the stronger Korea-style capability-building claim.
- Formalize the rent-allocation/capture mechanism only if it produces a distinct proposition rather than decorative notation.
- Investigate whether the expected withdrawal rule changes investment in capability in a tractable dynamic model.
- Keep worker-transition policy outside the core theorem unless it can be linked cleanly to political sustainability of reform.
